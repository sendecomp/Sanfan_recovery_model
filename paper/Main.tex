\begin{abstract}
This paper proposes a stochastic model that elucidates the race between fault propagation and recovery actions in a networked system. The basis of the model is a time-dependent Markov decision process model that predicts the failure and recovery of components and enables comparison of different recovery schemes with respect to one or more aspects of system survivability. 
Efficacy of the proposed method is illustrated through case studies on smart grids based on the IEEE-14 and IEEE-30 test systems, using high-fidelity digital twins that accurately reflect component states and cascading dynamics. The results demonstrate that the model supports objective comparison of recovery strategies under consistent survivability criteria.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
stochastic recovery model, Markov decision process, failure propagation, decision support, cascading failure, survivability
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Cascading failures pose a significant hazard to the survivability of interconnected complex systems. They can delay restoration, increase costs, and cause extended service interruptions. Predicting these consequences requires analytical frameworks that capture the dynamics of failure propagation and reflect the progression of repair. This paper seeks to elucidate the race between failure propagation and recovery actions intended to restore the system's functionality. We propose a mathematical model that helps identify the most prudent sequence of recovery actions given a specific objective, such as maximizing service capacity.

Related studies assume that recovery is undertaken when the system has been shut down for repair or isolation mechanisms are in place to prevent failure propagation. This is an unrealistic assumption for critical systems, as demonstrated by the blackouts in Kenya and Spain. Our work also addresses another common shortcoming of related studies - the assumption that recovery times are fixed or exponentially distributed \cite{sarkale_solving_2018, biswas_repair_2025}. Given that recovery is neither deterministic nor memoryless, our model better reflects reality by allowing any probability distribution to be used for recovery times. The likelihood of failure propagation is highest when the triggering failure occurs and declines throughout the recovery process, as failed components block coupling paths or the system adapts to mitigate or isolate failure. Time-dependent state transitions allow our model to reflect these aspects of failure and recovery.

\subsection{Related work in recovery modeling}

The second category of related literature is comprised of studies that propose, evaluate, or compare recovery schemes for complex networked systems, with the ultimate goal of identifying the quickest, least costly, or otherwise ``best'' scheme. Notable work in this category includes studies by Yan et al. \cite{yan_dynamic_2021} and Sarkale et al. \cite{sarkale_solving_2018}, where an MDP model has been applied to physical (not cyber-physical) power and water systems, respectively, with the goal of identifying the dynamic recovery scheme with the highest recovery rate.  
In the former method, the extent of lost functionality is the basis for estimating the performance, and the scheme chosen is the one that recovers the most functionality. In the latter, the number of users who continue to receive service is the basis for comparison. A related model by Biswas et al. \cite{biswas_repair_2025} assumes a post-hazard scenario where the exact location of the failure is unknown and crews have to search approximate failure areas before they can begin repair. This adds a path-finding problem to the recovery scheme. Lin et al. \cite{lin_condition-based_2022} propose an  MDP model for the maintenance of power supply equipment, where the goal is to minimize the sum of penalties and repair costs and reduce the cost of upkeep in the long run.

The closest work to ours is by Wang et al. \cite{wang_q-learning-based_2025}, who propose a model and a Q-learning-based recovery method for a cyber-physical power system. Their model was developed to analyze the coupling between components within a Cyber-Physical Production System and to forecast potential failures before the initiation of recovery protocols. The work identifies potential cascading failures, but allows the cascade to complete before beginning recovery efforts. This approach delays recovery and fails to capture the race that is the focus of our work.

A fundamental distinction of our work is in recognizing the non-deterministic nature of recovery times, yet allowing for any probability distribution to be assumed. The model by Wang et al. \cite{wang_q-learning-based_2025} makes the unrealistic assumption of equal duration for all recovery tasks. Biswas et al. \cite{biswas_repair_2025} model assume fixed, known recovery times, which are similarly unlikely. These simplistic assumptions may be adequate for coarse time scales (measured in days). The urgency of preventing failure propagation necessitates a finer timescale (minutes or hours), where recovery actions are unlikely to be of deterministic duration. The model by Sarkale et al. ~\cite{sarkale_solving_2018} acknowledges this uncertainty but assumes exponentially distributed recovery times. Given that a complex system can include diverse cyber and physical components, it is likely that recovery time distributions for different components will be similarly diverse. For example, for electrical components that deliver power to the physical layer of a smart grid, recovery times typically reflect a lognormal ~\cite{l_c_cadwallader_review_2012} distributions. However, in the same smart grid, failed cyber components such as smart meters are typically recovered by rebooting, which is quick (<5 mins) enough that the recovery time can be approximated with an exponential distribution. Our model allows for inhomogeneous recovery‑time distributions, affording the flexibility necessary to represent structurally and functionally complex networked systems across diverse domains.

This section articulates the formulation of our Markov Decision Process (MDP) recovery model, where time‑dependent transitions allow for accurate reflection of  interdependencies and enable consideration of recovery efforts of uncertain duration. The goal is to characterize failure and recovery efforts in complex networked systems by predicting the respective states of components and repair crews, tracking failure propagation and recovery, assessing impacts on system survivability, and evaluating competing recovery schemes. 

\subsection{Assumptions}

The following assumptions underlie our model:

\begin{enumerate}
    
    \item \textbf{Failure Dynamics:} In each decision epoch, multiple components may fail or recover as failures occur, propagate through network interdependencies, and are resolved through recovery efforts. Failure propagation is represented as a stochastic process determined by component-specific failure rates and interdependencies among system elements,  enabling a realistic depiction of cascading failures at the system level. \label{assumption failure}

    \item \textbf{Failure Scope:} At the current stage, the model focuses exclusively on cascading failure dynamics triggered by specific initial failure scenarios. Random component failures that are independent of but concurrent with an ongoing cascade are outside the scope of the present study and may be incorporated in future work.\label{assumption scope}
    
    \item \textbf{Observability of components:} The state (operational or failed) of each system component is assumed to be observable to the decision maker, either directly or through sensor outputs transmitted to the control center. This is a reasonable assumption in modern systems with adequate diagnostics in place. Several recent studies have demonstrated the utility of wireless communication, sensors, and monitoring systems in detecting component-level failures in a smart system \cite{aust_performance_2012, verma_wireless_2014, tashtarian_distributed_2018}. \label{assumption:obser}

    \item \textbf{General distribution for recovery times:} The duration of a given recovery action is estimated using empirical distributions derived from historical maintenance records, incident reports, and operational logs. Our model does not constrain the probability distribution of any recovery action, and allow for variability arising from severity of failure, efficiency of resource allocation, and environmental conditions. Recovery time for a given component includes logistics, repair, boot‑up, verification, and testing, as all steps require the continued presence of crews at the component site.
     \label{assumption:repairt}
    
    \item \textbf{Success of recovery:} Any failed component is assumed to be recoverable, with restoration always succeeding through either repair or replacement. This simplifying assumption enables clear interpretation of results and can be relaxed in future extensions to the work.

    \item  \textbf{Recovery resources:} A fixed number of repair crews are assumed to be available throughout the entire recovery process. Each crew is assumed to be capable of executing any required repair or replacement task, but is limited to one assignment at a time, establishing a realistic yet tractable representation of resource constraints.
\end{enumerate}

Collectively, these assumptions establish a modeling framework that enables a more realistic representation of a complex networked system while preserving the essential dynamics of failure propagation and recovery. At the same time, these assumptions highlight opportunities for future model extensions, allowing gradual relaxation of assumptions as data and computational resources permit.

\subsection{Model formulation}
The foundation of our recovery model is an MDP, which is a state-based model that reflects sequential decision-making when actions can have uncertain outcomes. An MDP has four elements: a state space, a set of actions, a set of transition probabilities that govern the result of an action, and a set of rewards that assign value to these results. The goal of an MDP is to identify a sequence of actions that maximizes some cumulative function of the reward. 

\subsubsection{System state, \texorpdfstring{$\mathbf{s}$}{S}}
In our model, the state occupied by the MDP at a given decision epoch is determined by the respective states of the components and the crews.

The \emph{components} are defined at a granularity that allows the state — either \emph{operational} or \emph{failed} -  of each component to be directly observable, consistent with Assumption \ref{assumption:obser} (Observability of Components). If a component has failed, the failure time is recorded in the state of the component. This enables calculation of time-dependent transitions. The state variable, $x_i$, of a given component, $i$, is assigned as in Equation \ref{eq:xs}. All components are assumed to be functional at startup, i.e., $t_{fi}>0$ for all $i$.
\begin{equation} x_i (t)= \begin{cases}
    0 & \text{if component \textit{i} is operational}  \\
    t_{fi} & \text{if component \textit{i} has failed at time $t_{fi}$}
    \label{eq:xs}
\end{cases}\end{equation}

For a complex networked system with a total of $N$ components, the set of component states is represented by the vector $\mathbf{x}$, as in Equation \ref{eq:X}, where $x_i(t)$ is the state of component $i$ at time $t$. 
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x} (t) = \{ x_1(t), x_2(t), \ldots , x_N(t) \}
\label{eq:X}
\end{equation}

Recovery crews are the second aspect involved in identifying the state of the recovery process at a given decision epoch. Each crew, $k$, can be either \emph{idle} or \emph{busy} recovering a particular component. The corresponding state, $y_k$, can be assigned as shown in Equation \ref{eq:ys}, where $i$ represents the failed component that is assigned to crew $k$ for recovery. 
\begin{equation} y_k (t)= \begin{cases}
    0 & \text{idle, no recovery task assigned} \\
    i & \text{taking recovery actions on component $i$}
    \label{eq:ys}
\end{cases}\end{equation}

Assuming $M$ crews are available for dispatch, the status of the crews is collectively represented by the vector $\mathbf{y}$, as in Equation \ref{eq:Y}, where $y_k(t)$ is the status of crew $k$ at time $t$. 

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}(t) = \{ y_1(t), y_2(t), \ldots , y_M(t) \}
\label{eq:Y}
\end{equation}

Other than tracking the failed component that each crew is recovering, the time remaining to complete the recovery task is also tracked in a vector, $\mathbf{z}$, of length $M$. Each $z_k$ is an integer that represents the remaining recovery time for crew $k$, as in Equation \ref{eq:z}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}(t) = \{z_1(t), z_2(t), z_3(t), .., z_M(t)\}
    \label{eq:z}
\end{equation}


Combining the component states in Equation \ref{eq:X} and the crew states in equations \ref{eq:Y} and \ref{eq:z}, we obtain the system state, as illustrated in Equation \ref{eq:S}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{s}(t) = \{\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{y}(t), \mathbf{z}(t) \}
    \label{eq:S}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Actions, \texorpdfstring{$\mathbf{A}$}{A}}
%How to address general repair distribution with TI
The action space, $\mathbf{A}$ is the set of actions available to the system at a given decision epoch. A given action can cause the system to transition from one state to another. Recommending an action comprises decision support for the recovery process. The action space is finite and countable; each action represents assigning the recovery of a failed component ($i | x_i \neq 0$)  to an idle crew ($k | y_k = 0$). 

At each decision epoch, recovery of one failed component will be assigned to each idle crew, based on the given recovery scheme. In this paper, we assume that recovery of a component involves exactly one crew, i.e., for every $x_i \in \mathbf{x}$ where $x_i \neq 0$, there is at most one $y_k \in \mathbf{y}$ where $y_k = i$. The assignment of component $i$ to crew $k$ will cause $y_k$ to transition in the next time step from ``idle'' to $i$, reflecting the assignment. The corresponding recovery time tracker, $z_k$, will be assigned a new value drawn from the recovery time distribution of $i$. Scheduling of recovery actions is non-preemptive; i.e., a crew will remain on a given task until it is complete. 

\subsubsection{Time-dependent transition probabilities, \texorpdfstring{$P_a$}{P}}
In an MDP, transition $p_{a_t}(s,s')$ represents the probability that taking action $a$ in state $s$ at time $t$ will cause the system to transition to state $s'$ at the next decision epoch (time $t+1$). In our model, the transitions are governed by two different processes: failure propagation and recovery efforts. Each type of transition, respectively, is explained described below.

\paragraph{Failure propagation} 
A component can transition from ``operational''  to  ``failed'' due to independent failure or dependency on a failed component. The probability of the latter can be quantified by a \emph{dependency index}. For a system with $N$ components, the dependency indices can be represented by a matrix, $\mathbf{D}$, as in Equation \ref{eq:D}, where $d_{ij}$ represents the conditional probability of component $j$ failing one time step after component $i$ has failed. :
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{D}=[d_{ij} ] \in [0,1]^{N \times N}  \label{eq:D}
\end{equation}
$$i \in [1, N], j \in [1, N]$$ 
In earlier work \cite{redacted}, we proposed a method based on heuristic causation analysis to compute the value of each element of $\mathbf{D}$. Other measures of dependency include Pearson correlation ~\cite{modarres_measures_2011}, Spearman's rank correlation ~\cite{modarres_measures_2011} , and the randomized dependence coefficient ~\cite{lopez-paz_randomized_2013}, among others. The recovery model we propose in this paper is agnostic to the measure chosen to quantify dependency. 

Proceeding with the method proposed in \cite{redacted}, we normalize $\mathbf{D}$ by dividing it by $N$ to ensure that $\sum^{n}_{j=1}d_{ij} \leq 1$. The matrix $\left( \frac{1}{N} \mathbf{D} \right)^l$ is then used to predict the probability of failure propagation from each component to every other component after $l$ time steps. Given the state of component $i$, the probability that component $j$ will fail in the next time step in a cascade from $i$ is the conditional probability shown in Equation \ref{eq:pij}.
\begin{equation}
p_{ij}(t) = \begin{cases}
    0 & x_i = 0 \\
    d_{ij} \in \left( \frac{1}{n} \mathbf{D} \right)^{(t-x_i)}    &x_i \neq 0
\end{cases}
\label{eq:pij}
\end{equation}
In this equation, $t$ represents the current time. $x_i$ is the failure time of the component $i$, as described in Equation \ref{eq:xs}. 
To find the total cascading effect on component $j$, a summation from every component in the system must be calculated, as shown in Equation \ref{eq:c}, which represents the time-dependent transition probability for component $j$ from operational $(x_i(t) = 0)$ to failure $(x_i(t+1) = t+1)$ as a result of the cascade.

\begin{equation}
p_j(t)= \sum_{i=1}^{N} p_{ij}(t)
    \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Component recovery} Completion of recovery for component $i$ causes its state, $x_i$, to transition from failed $(x_i(t) =t_{fi})$ to operational $(x_i(t+z_k) = 0)$ via recovery efforts by crew $k$. As stated in assumption \ref{assumption:repairt}, recovery efforts are assumed to be successful; however, the time required for recovery is uncertain. Our recovery model does not constrain the distribution of recovery time for any component; any well-defined probability distribution can be assumed. As described in Assumption \ref{assumption:repairt}, the distribution is typically inferred from documentation such as incident reports. 

The remaining recovery time for the component, $z_k$, is decremented at each decision epoch, i.e.,  $z_k(t+1) = z_k(t) - 1$. When recovery is complete ($z_k$ = 0), the component is recovered ($x_i = 0$), and crew $k$ enters the idle state ($y_k = 0$) and becomes available for a new assignment. 

\subsubsection{Rewards \texorpdfstring{$\mathbf{R_a}$}{R}}
\label{sec:rewards}
The set of rewards, $\mathbf{R_a}$, represent the ``value'' or ``cost'' associated with the actions available in an MDP. $r_a(s,s')$ represents the reward received after action $a$ causes the system to transition from state $s$ to state $s'$ at the next decision epoch. The goal of an MDP is to identify a ``policy'',  i.e., a sequence of actions, that optimizes a given cumulative function of rewards. 

One goal of our model is to enable comparison of different recovery schemes (policies) based on one or more measures associated with system resilience. One commonly used measure is \emph{recovery time} \cite{biswas_repair_2025}, which refers to the duration of recovery efforts required for every system component to become operational. Another measure is the extent of \emph{failure propagation} \cite{wang_q-learning-based_2025}, assessed by calculating the total number of additional failures triggered by the initial failure. Yet another measure is the fraction of full \emph{service capacity} \cite{redacted3,yan_dynamic_2021,sarkale_solving_2018} delivered by the system in a given state. Full service capacity is assumed to be delivered when all components are operational. Finally, recovery schemes can be compared based on their ability to meet specified \emph{recovery deadlines}. This measure often applies to critical systems such as power grids, where prolonged service outage can carry severe consequences. Perishable food items in supermarkets may begin to spoil within a few hours. Residential buildings can lose their internal thermal stability within approximately 8–12 hours in cold climates \cite{noauthor_how_nodate}. Under extreme conditions, such loss of heating can be life-threatening, as evidenced by the fatalities reported during the 2021 Texas power crisis~\cite{svitek_texas_2022}.

\section{Design of Case Study and Experimental Setup}\label{sec:design}
To demonstrate that our recovery model effectively captures the dynamics of a complex networked system, particularly the competition between failure propagation and recovery efforts, we present case studies involving smart grids. This application domain was selected to build on our previous work, and because of its relevance, clarity, and scalability. Our experiments involve respective smart grids created from the IEEE-14 and IEEE-30 test systems, which include physical components such as power generators, buses, and transmission lines. To convert each conventional grid to its smart counterpart, we supplemented it with a cyber layer that incorporates phasor measurement units (PMUs) that are high-speed sensors for monitoring of voltages and currents \cite{asprou_optimal_2011}, flexible AC transmission systems (FACTS) that control power flow and increase stability \cite{sosnina_assessment_2022, garrido_location_2019}, and  decision support that determines optimal settings for the FACTS device based on data from the PMUs. 

We evaluated four recovery schemes against representative failure scenarios commonly observed in these test systems. Among the physical components, the focus of our case studies is on  transmission lines, as they typically have higher failure rates than other components and are often the cause of power outages \cite{song_survey_2013}. Our failure scenarios involve all three types of cyber components, i.e., PMUs, FACTS devices, and the decision support system, as our previous work has demonstrated that their malfunction can lead to cascading failure in an otherwise stable grid \cite{redacted2}. In the remainder of this section, we describe the design and setup of the experiments in detail. 

\subsection{Recovery schemes}
We demonstrate the utility of our model by using it to compare the four recovery schemes summarized in Table \ref{table:recovery} and described below. These schemes differ in how they prioritize components for recovery.

\begin{description}
    \item[Randomized:] At each decision epoch, a failed component is selected at random and assigned to an available crew for recovery. 
    
    \item[Pre-computed:] Components are prioritized for recovery based on their rank in a static list that is computed beforehand. Measures commonly used for ranking components include the number of functional dependencies on a component or the service capacity lost as a result of its failure. The importance measure used in our case studies is its \emph{criticality}, defined as the cumulative effect of its failure on other components \cite{redacted5} and computed from the dependency matrix, $\mathbf{D}$, as in Equation \ref{eq:precomputed}.
    \begin{equation}
        \text{criticality of component } i = \mathbf{D}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{N} d_{ij}
        \label{eq:precomputed}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item[Max-capacity:] Components are dynamically prioritized based on a method we adapted from Yan at el. \cite{yan_dynamic_2021}. Our scheme aims to increase service resilience by prioritizing components whose recovery leaves the system with the highest survivability. In this context, survivability is defined as the fraction of full service capacity delivered by the system during degraded operation. 
    
    \item[Min-cascade:] This scheme aims to lower the overall dependencies in the system by recovering components that have a high likelihood of causing other failures, yet can typically be recovered quickly. Components are dynamically prioritized for recovery based on their \emph{cascading index}, which is computed as in Equation \ref{eq:min_cascade}.
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:min_cascade}
    \text{cascading index of } i 
    = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N} p_{ij} (t)}{E[t_{r_i}]}
    \end{equation}
\end{description}



\subsection{Simulation environment}
%SSS - mention that data sets, code, etc. are publicly available for reuse
Our experiments were carried out using fault injection on PSAT ~\cite{psat_2005}, an open-source MATLAB-based toolbox for power system analysis, which we have modified to allow simulation of interactions between cyber components such as PMUs and the decision support algorithm that identifies the settings for the FACTS devices. Paper \cite{redacted4, redacted}  describes the simulation environment and explains how cyber-physical interdependencies are quantified. 

The recovery model was implemented in Python 3 on Google Colab \cite{colab}, with three inputs: i) the dependency matrix, ii) the recovery time distribution for each component, and iii) a means of assessing system survivability. 

The \emph{dependency matrix}, $(\mathbf{D})$, governs the transition probabilities and reflects cascading failure. As described earlier, dependency indices can be computed using a variety of methods. For our experiment, the respective dependency matrices for the IEEE-14 and 30 smart grids were calculated using the method we proposed in \cite{redacted}. %For better observe of the failure propagation in the system, we amplify the matrix calculated by $\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{D}^\frac{1}{2.5}$, so the value will exceed 1.

The \emph{recovery time} for transmission lines was assumed to range from three to seven hours, following a lognormal distribution. This is based on recent studies on the repair time of physical components \cite{zapata_repair_2008, arif_optimizing_2018}. For smart devices, the total recovery duration is assumed to be exponentially distributed between 1 and 3 hours. The US Department of Energy reports that such components can be remotely rebooted in fewer than five minutes \cite{noauthor_recovery_nodate}. Our larger values account for overhead time in preparation, travel, and installation for cases where remote recovery is impossible or the component has to be replaced \cite{arif_optimizing_2018}.

%SSS - add a citation for EN-50160. Or delete everything after adequate power. 
A fundamental function required of a smart grid is the reliable delivery of uninterrupted electrical power to subscribers. In our experiments, survivability was assessed as the fraction of power loads in the system that receive adequate power. Consistent with standards such as EN-50160, we determine whether a load is considered supplied by verifying that the voltage of the bus to which the load is connected is within a specified admissible interval. As an illustration, EN-50160 defines this interval as 0.9 to 1.1 per unit, where the per-unit quantity corresponds to normalization regarding a reference value, in this context a nominal voltage.

The termination criteria for recovery simulations were full system restoration or reaching a predefined simulation horizon. The finite horizon reflects practical recovery windows and avoids unrealistically long recovery efforts. The percentage of runs that complete recovery within the horizon is recorded as the \emph{coverage} of the scheme. For incomplete runs, the duration of the horizon was recorded as the earliest restoration time.

Of the two smart grids analyzed in our case studies, the IEEE‑14 test system is used for detailed examination of the recovery process. The small size of the grid facilitates comparison of multiple scenarios across multiple simulations. Experiments on the IEEE‑30 test system demonstrate scalability of our model.

For scenarios involving up to three failures, a lookup table was used to determine system survivability. Given that the average home stays warm for 8 to 12 hours after a power outage \cite{noauthor_how_nodate}), a recovery deadline of 12 hours was set for reaching 90\% survivability.

The locations were identified by Acharya and Mithulananthan  ~\cite{acharya_locating_2007} as achieving the highest stability in voltage and power factor. \subsection{Experiment 3: Extreme weather (Massive physical failure)}
On rare occasions, power grids can experience massive physical failure due to extreme weather events, such as hurricanes, wildfires, or earthquakes. The 2021 North American winter storm~\cite{menati_preliminary_2021} and the 2016 South Australian storm~\cite{jamborsalamati_framework_2018} are  examples where extreme weather caused catastrophic blackouts. Although these events are rare, the severity of their consequences makes them work examining. In situations involving extreme weather, the probability of failure propagation could increase, as components become more vulnerable under such conditions. Recovery times are likely to be longer, as crews have to operate difficult circumstances. As failures typically outnumber available crews during extreme weather, dispatch decision schemes become critical.

\subsection{Experiment 4: Two-line failure in IEEE-30}
Our experiments with the IEEE-30 test system are intended to demonstrate scalability of the proposed model. The original IEEE-30 grid has 41 lines, 30 buses, and 24 loads. Similar to the IEEE-14 case, we created the smart IEEE-30 grid depicted in Figure \ref{fig:IEEE30} by adding PMUs, FACTS devices, and a decision support system to the original IEEE-30 test system. 
%We have created IEEE-30 smart from the original system by adding 6 PMUs on BUS 1,5,10,12,15,27; and 5 FACTS on lines between BUS 6 \& 8, 8 \& 28, 22 \& 28, 27 \& 28, 27 \& 30; for the best observability and configurability. 
 We use the same method as the first three experiments to calculate dependency indices for IEEE-30. We assume the same recovery time distribution for each type of component. The only difference is in the system survivability calculation. MATLAB with the PSAT toolbox was embedded into Google Colab to compute system survivability for when the system has fewer than three failed lines; the survivability is assumed to be zero otherwise (as in the IEEE-14 experiments). Using the PSAT toolbox is a compromise to the size of IEEE-30. Since there are 41 lines, 5 FACTS devices, and a decision support system, there could be a $2^{46} \approx 10^{13}$ unique states. The resulting lookup table would be prohibitively large. The downside of using embedded PSAT is the execution time (typically 10 seconds per check), which is considerably slower than using a search table ($< 1$ second per check). 
 Given the computational constraints, we limited this experiment to 100 trials and 50 time steps horizon.%Perhaps artificial intelligence techniques could be used to speed up the power flow calculation in our future work. 

To systematically capture the race between failure propagation and recovery efforts, we examined a double-failure scenario for the IEEE-30 smart grid. This system is larger and has more structural redundancy than the IEEE-14, and its inter-component dependencies are comparatively weaker; as a result, the failure of a single component is more readily absorbed by the network. Consequently, we select a two-component initial failure for our experimental setup. From the 53 candidate components in the system, we designate transmission lines 23 and 6 (see Figure \ref{fig:IEEE30}) as the initial failure. This choice is informed by importance analysis performed on all IEEE-30 components using the method introduced in \cite{redacted5}, where these two lines were found to be  most likely to induce extensive fault propagation and exert the largest adverse effect on system survivability. As in the first three experiments, we amplified the dependency matrix to increase the likelihood of observing cascading events.

The result of this experiment is unexpectedly different from any IEEE-14 result. Since the IEEE-30 is larger and more complex than the IEEE-14, we examined the state transitions within the simulation and discovered an interesting phenomenon. As we increase the number of repair crews, the additional failures and earliest restoration time increase in some cases,  especially when the Pre-computed recovery scheme is used. We attribute this phenomenon to failure propagation. Some components have much stronger dependencies (higher likelihood of failing or causing failure); strong dependencies would take longer for the system to adapt and absorb. Equation \ref{eq:pij} shows that dependencies are decreasing exponentially with time. However, if a weak-dependency component repeatedly fails and is recovered, the system will have to repeatedly absorb the failures. It will cause more additional failures than leaving the component failed. A similar phenomenon was observed in the 2023 Kenya blackout, where repairs failed to alleviate the power shortage, yet expanded the outage to additional regions. The Pre-computed scheme appears to aggravate this phenomenon and achieved worse outcomes when a greater number of crews were available. As shown in Table \ref{TB:30results}, both additional failures and restoration time increase, and the coverage decreases. Figure \ref{fig:30-result}) shows that the recovery rate of Pre-computed  worsens over time.

In examining the state transitions, we observed that Min-cascade recovered the smart devices before the lines. An operational cyber layer redirects the power flow to a stable state. Max-capacity prioritized the physical layer, recovering the lines first. This will provide sufficient power to the remining part of the system and reduced additional failures among them. The Randomized scheme is similar to the Pre-computed scheme, in its lack of focus on one layer of the system. Haphazard recovery actions can leave failure propagation paths open, leading to many additional failures. However, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:30-result}, the Randomized scheme was able to restore the greatest survivability before the deadline (time step 16). It also achieves higher average survivability before the 20th time step. Min-cascade dominates the rest of the timeline, similar to the Pre-computed scheme in the three IEEE-14 experiments. 

The main purpose of the fourth experiment is to demonstrate scalability of the model by applying it to a larger and more complex power grid as compared to the IEEE-14. Simulation time can be a revealing measure. The IEEE-30 experiment takes much longer than any of the IEEE-14 experiment, because a survivability lookup table would be prohibitively large and survivability has to be computed with PSAT. Simulation of the IEEE-14 with Google Colab takes between 30 minutes and one hour, with runtime type set to CPU, using 1-2 GB of RAM and 20-25 GB of disk. Simulating the IEEE-30 in the same environment requires between two and four hours, but requires roughly the same amount of memory and disk space. If the survivability computation were eliminated from the IEEE-30 experiment, 100 trials of the simulation would complete in approximately 12 minutes. Comparing this to the 30 minutes to one hour required for 1000 trials of the IEEE-14 simulation demonstrates that the model scales well.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper proposes a mathematical model to analyze the race between cascading failure and recovery efforts in a complex networked system. Our model predicts failure propagation with consideration of the effect of recovery actions and facilitates evaluation of system survivability after each action, all of which are crucial to decision support during recovery.

To illustrate the utility of our model, we carried out case studies on respective simulated smart grids based on the IEEE-14 and IEEE-30 test systems. Given a number of recovery schemes that differ in objective, for example maintaining service capacity vs. limiting failure propagation vs. achieving the fastest recovery, the experiments collectively demonstrated that our model can provide decision support during recovery by elucidating differences between their outcomes.

Our model is not limited to power grids. Given the topology and component-level information, our model can be used to analyze recovery schemes for other networked systems. Examples include water distribution networks and transportation systems. Other directions for extending the model to include consideration of partially observable component states, and concurrent component failures that are independent of an ongoing cascade. More refined repair time distributions that incorporate logistics-based time estimation, as well as allowing for partially or completely unsuccessful recovery actions, are among potential enhancements to the model.
